## 1. Introduction to Journal Entries

### Meaning
Every day, a business performs dozens of financial transactions—buying goods, paying shop rent, selling products to customers, depositing cash into a bank, or paying employee salaries. A **Journal Entry** is the systematic, first-hand written record of a business transaction in the books of accounts, showing which account is **debited** and which account is **credited**, along with the exact date, amount, and a brief explanation.

### Why It Is Important
Human memory is limited. A shopkeeper cannot remember hundreds of cash receipts, credit purchases, and bills paid over an entire financial year. Recording journal entries ensures that every rupee entering or leaving the business is captured accurately the moment a transaction occurs. Without journal entries, a business cannot prepare Ledgers, a Trial Balance, or Final Financial Statements (Profit & Loss Account and Balance Sheet).

### Simple Example
Suppose you open a small stationery shop in Pune and invest ₹3,00,000 cash into the business. Instead of writing an informal note like *"Started shop with 3 lakhs"*, accounting requires a structured **Journal Entry** that clearly separates the two effects:
1. Cash comes into the business (₹3,00,000).
2. The owner becomes the giver of capital (₹3,00,000).

### Practical Accounting Application
In the **Accounting Cycle**, journalising is the very first formal recording step right after verifying source documents (such as cash memos, invoices, bank deposit slips, and rent receipts):

```text
Source Documents (Bills/Receipts)
        ↓
   [1. JOURNAL]  ← We are learning this step!
        ↓
   2. Ledger (Grouping by account)
        ↓
   3. Trial Balance (Checking arithmetic accuracy)
        ↓
   4. Final Accounts (Profit & Loss A/c and Balance Sheet)
```

## 2. Meaning and Importance of Journal

### Meaning
The word **Journal** comes from the French word *Jour*, meaning **"day"**. A Journal is therefore a daily record of business transactions arranged in **chronological order** (date-wise order, as transactions happen).

Because every transaction is recorded in the Journal first before being transferred (posted) to any other accounting book, the Journal is known as the **Book of Original Entry** (or **Book of Prime Entry**). The process of recording a transaction in a Journal is called **Journalising**.

### Why It Is Important
A Journal serves five essential functions in Financial Accounting:

1. **Chronological Record:** Transactions are recorded strictly date-wise, making it easy to trace what happened on any specific day.
2. **Complete Record at One Place:** Both the debit effect and the credit effect of a transaction appear together in a single entry, giving the full picture immediately.
3. **Built-in Explanation (Narration):** Every journal entry carries a short written explanation called a *Narration*, so anyone reading the books months later understands why the entry was passed.
4. **Error Reduction:** Because Total Debits must equal Total Credits for every single entry, arithmetical mistakes are caught immediately at the recording stage.
5. **Legal and Audit Evidence:** Supported by source vouchers and bills, a properly maintained Journal serves as reliable evidence for auditors, tax authorities, and courts.

### Simple Example
If **Mehta Stationery & Office Mart** pays electricity charges of ₹4,500 on April 5 and buys paper stock of ₹20,000 on April 6, the Journal records April 5 first and April 6 next—never out of sequence.

### Practical Accounting Application
In modern businesses using accounting software (like TallyPrime or Zoho Books), even though computers automate ledger posting, accountants still pass **Journal Vouchers** using the exact same Debit and Credit logic you are learning here.

## 3. Rules of Debit and Credit

### Meaning
Financial Accounting works on the **Double-Entry System**, which is rooted in the **Dual Aspect Principle**: *every business transaction affects at least two accounts equally and oppositely.*

To record these two sides uniformly, accounting uses two standard terms:
- **Debit (`Dr.`)** — the left-hand side of an account.
- **Credit (`Cr.`)** — the right-hand side of an account.

> **Important Beginner Clarification:** Do **not** think of Debit as "bad/loss" and Credit as "good/gain" in everyday language. In accounting, **Debit** and **Credit** are simply directional labels whose effect depends strictly on the **type of account** involved.

### Why It Is Important
The fundamental mathematical law of every journal entry is:

$$\text{Total Debit Amount (₹)} = \text{Total Credit Amount (₹)}$$

If a transaction has ₹50,000 on the Debit side, the Credit side must also total exactly ₹50,000. This self-balancing rule keeps the entire accounting system accurate.

### Simple Example
When a business pays ₹10,000 in cash to buy a printer:
- One aspect receives value: **Office Equipment** comes in → **Debit ₹10,000**.
- One aspect gives value: **Cash** goes out → **Credit ₹10,000**.
- Total Debit (₹10,000) = Total Credit (₹10,000).

### Practical Accounting Application
There are two internationally recognised methods for deciding which account to Debit and which to Credit:
1. **The Traditional Approach (English Approach):** Classifies accounts into *Personal, Real, and Nominal Accounts* and applies the *Three Golden Rules*. **(Primary framework taught in this chapter.)**
2. **The Modern Accounting Equation Approach (American Approach):** Classifies accounts into *Assets, Liabilities, Capital, Expenses, and Revenues*. *(Explained separately in Section 8.)*

## 4. Types of Accounts (Traditional Approach)

Under the **Traditional Approach**, every account in a business belongs to one of **three** categories:
1. **Personal Accounts**
2. **Real Accounts**
3. **Nominal Accounts**

```text
                       ACCOUNTS (Traditional Approach)
                                     │
         ┌───────────────────────────┼───────────────────────────┐
         ▼                           ▼                           ▼
  PERSONAL ACCOUNTS            REAL ACCOUNTS             NOMINAL ACCOUNTS
 (Persons & Entities)     (Assets & Properties)      (Expenses, Losses,
         │                           │                Incomes & Gains)
  ┌──────┼──────┐             ┌──────┴──────┐
  ▼      ▼      ▼             ▼             ▼
Natural Artif. Repr.       Tangible     Intangible
```

### 4.1 Personal Accounts
- **Meaning:** Accounts relating to **persons**—whether human beings, companies, banks, firms, institutions, or accounts representing a person or group of persons.
- **Why It Is Important:** Businesses constantly deal with people and organisations on credit (suppliers, customers, banks, and the owner). Personal accounts track who owes money to the business (Debtors) and whom the business owes money to (Creditors).
- **Simple Examples:** *Ramesh's A/c, Priya Enterprises A/c, State Bank of India A/c, Capital A/c, Drawings A/c, Outstanding Salary A/c.*
- **Practical Application:** When you buy goods on credit from *Saraswati Paper Mart Pvt. Ltd.*, you open their Personal Account to remember that you must pay them later.

### 4.2 Real Accounts
- **Meaning:** Accounts relating to **properties, assets, and possessions** owned by the business (excluding debtors/banks, which are Personal Accounts).
- **Why It Is Important:** Real accounts track the physical and non-physical economic resources of the business. Their closing balances are carried forward to the next year and appear in the **Balance Sheet**.
- **Simple Examples:** *Cash A/c, Furniture A/c, Machinery A/c, Building A/c, Goodwill A/c, Patents A/c.*
- **Practical Application:** When a factory buys a machine for ₹2,00,000, the Real Account (*Machinery A/c*) records this long-term asset owned by the business.

### 4.3 Nominal Accounts
- **Meaning:** Accounts relating to **expenses, losses, incomes, and gains** of the business. The word *Nominal* means "in name only"—these accounts do not represent physical assets or people; they exist during the year to measure profit or loss.
- **Why It Is Important:** Nominal accounts allow the business to calculate its net profit or net loss at year-end. Unlike Real and Personal accounts, Nominal accounts are closed at the end of the financial year by transferring their balances to the **Trading and Profit & Loss Account**.
- **Simple Examples:** *Salary A/c, Rent A/c, Purchases A/c, Sales A/c, Electricity Charges A/c, Commission Received A/c, Discount Allowed A/c, Depreciation A/c.*
- **Practical Application:** Recording *Rent A/c*, *Salary A/c*, and *Sales A/c* separately helps a shop owner see whether revenue exceeded operating expenses during the year.

> **Accounting Note on Purchases A/c and Sales A/c:** In modern B.Com and ICAI Financial Accounting curricula, **Purchases A/c** (cost of buying goods for resale) and **Sales A/c** (revenue from selling goods) are classified as **Nominal Accounts** because they represent expense and revenue and close to the Trading Account at year-end. We follow this consistent classification throughout this entire course.

## 5. Natural, Artificial, and Representative Personal Accounts

Personal Accounts are further divided into **three subtypes**. Knowing these subtypes prevents the most common exam mistakes!

| Subtype | Meaning | Why It Matters | Simple Examples | Practical Business Application |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Natural Personal Accounts** | Accounts of **living human beings** created by nature. | Tracks amounts receivable from or payable to individual customers, suppliers, or employees. | *Arjun's A/c, Meera's A/c, Ramesh Verma's A/c* | Recording ₹15,000 credit sales made to an individual customer, *Arjun*. |
| **2. Artificial Personal Accounts** | Accounts of **legal entities created by law**—companies, partnership firms, banks, colleges, clubs, and government bodies. They have no physical human body, yet the law treats them as "persons" who can enter contracts and hold bank accounts. | Essential because businesses transact mostly with banks and registered companies rather than individuals. **Crucially, Bank A/c is always an Artificial Personal Account (NOT a Real Account).** | *Bank A/c, State Bank of India A/c, HDFC Bank A/c, Infosys Ltd. A/c, Bharat Suppliers Pvt. Ltd. A/c* | Recording a cheque deposit into *Bank A/c* or credit purchase from *Saraswati Paper Mart Pvt. Ltd.* |
| **3. Representative Personal Accounts** | Accounts that do not bear a person's name directly, but **represent a specific person or group of persons** behind the scenes. | Used for the owner's equity accounts and year-end accrual/prepayment adjustments. Whenever a prefix or suffix like **Outstanding, Prepaid, Accrued, or Unearned** is added to a Nominal Account, it transforms into a **Representative Personal Account**! | *Capital A/c* (represents owner), *Drawings A/c* (represents owner), *Outstanding Salary A/c* (represents employees), *Prepaid Insurance A/c* (represents insurance company), *Accrued Commission A/c* (represents party owing commission) | At year-end on March 31, if ₹24,000 staff salary is unpaid, *Outstanding Salary A/c* represents the employees to whom the business owes money. |

### Quick Memory Trick for Representative Personal Accounts
Look at how a Nominal Account changes when a timing word is attached:
- `Salary A/c` → **Nominal Account** (Expense)
- `Outstanding Salary A/c` → **Representative Personal Account** (Liability representing employees)
- `Insurance A/c` → **Nominal Account** (Expense)
- `Prepaid Insurance A/c` → **Representative Personal Account** (Asset representing the insurer who owes coverage)
- `Commission Received A/c` → **Nominal Account** (Income)
- `Accrued Commission A/c` → **Representative Personal Account** (Asset representing the party who owes earned commission)

## 6. Tangible and Intangible Real Accounts

Real Accounts (assets and properties) are divided into **two subtypes** based on physical existence:

| Subtype | Meaning | Why It Matters | Simple Examples | Practical Business Application |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Tangible Real Accounts** | Assets and properties that have a **physical form**—they can be seen, touched, felt, and measured physically. | Represents the physical infrastructure and cash needed for daily business operations. Most tangible fixed assets (except land) lose value over time through **Depreciation**. | *Cash A/c, Furniture A/c, Machinery A/c, Building A/c, Land A/c, Delivery Van A/c, Office Equipment A/c* | Buying a ₹1,40,000 embroidery machine for a garment unit is recorded in *Machinery A/c* (Tangible Real). |
| **2. Intangible Real Accounts** | Valuable business assets and legal rights that have **no physical existence**—they cannot be seen or touched, yet they hold real monetary value and help the business earn profits. | Protects a company's brand reputation, inventions, and intellectual property, and accurately reflects total business worth. | *Goodwill A/c, Patents A/c, Trademarks A/c, Copyrights A/c, Computer Software A/c* | Paying ₹2,50,000 to acquire the registered *Patent* for a unique water-filter design is recorded in *Patents A/c* (Intangible Real). |

> **Key Distinction:** Both *Cash A/c* (Tangible Real) and *Bank A/c* (Artificial Personal) hold your business's money, yet under the Traditional Approach they belong to **different account classes**! Always remember:
> - **Cash A/c** = Tangible Real Account (physical currency notes & coins you can touch).
> - **Bank A/c** = Artificial Personal Account (a banking institution holding your funds).

## 7. Three Golden Rules of Accounting

### Meaning
The **Three Golden Rules of Accounting** are the universal decision rules of the Traditional Approach. Once you know whether an account is Personal, Real, or Nominal, these three short rules immediately tell you whether to **Debit** or **Credit** it.

| Account Type | Golden Rule for DEBIT (`Dr.`) | Golden Rule for CREDIT (`Cr.`) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Personal Account** | **Debit the Receiver** | **Credit the Giver** |
| **2. Real Account** | **Debit what Comes In** | **Credit what Goes Out** |
| **3. Nominal Account** | **Debit all Expenses and Losses** | **Credit all Incomes and Gains** |

### Why They Are Important
Memorising individual journal entries is impossible because there are thousands of possible business situations. Mastering just these **three lines** empowers you to solve any journal entry logically from first principles.

### Simple Examples & Practical Applications of Each Rule

1. **Personal Account Rule (*Debit the Receiver, Credit the Giver*):**
   - *Example:* You deposit ₹50,000 cash into State Bank of India. The bank is the **receiver** of money → **Debit Bank A/c**.
   - *Example:* You buy goods worth ₹30,000 on credit from *Apex Traders*. Apex Traders is the **giver** of goods → **Credit Apex Traders A/c**.
   - *Owner Transactions:* Because the business and owner are separate entities, when the owner invests cash, the owner is the **giver** → **Credit Capital A/c**. When the owner withdraws cash or goods for personal use, the owner is the **receiver** → **Debit Drawings A/c**.

2. **Real Account Rule (*Debit what Comes In, Credit what Goes Out*):**
   - *Example:* You buy office furniture for ₹25,000 cash. Furniture **comes in** → **Debit Furniture A/c**; Cash **goes out** → **Credit Cash A/c**.
   - *Example (Depreciation):* When machinery wears out by ₹8,000 during the year, that portion of asset value effectively **goes out** (reduces) → **Credit Machinery A/c**.

3. **Nominal Account Rule (*Debit all Expenses and Losses, Credit all Incomes and Gains*):**
   - *Example:* You pay ₹12,000 staff salary in cash. Salary is an **expense** → **Debit Salary A/c**.
   - *Example:* You sell goods for ₹40,000 cash. Sales is a **revenue/income** → **Credit Sales A/c**.
   - *Reversal of an Expense (Special Application):* When a previously recorded expense needs to be **reduced**—for example, when goods originally bought for resale are withdrawn by the owner for personal use (*Drawings A/c Dr. To Purchases A/c*), or when part of an insurance premium paid relates to next year (*Prepaid Insurance A/c Dr. To Insurance A/c*)—we **Credit** the Nominal expense account to reduce that expense.

## 8. Modern Accounting Equation Approach

> **Important Pedagogical Note:** This section explains the **Modern Approach** completely separately from the Traditional Approach. Never mix labels from both methods in the same explanation (for instance, do not write *"Cash is a Real Asset Account"*). In this course, we use the **Traditional Approach** as our main framework for worked examples and exercises, while understanding the Modern Approach as a valuable second perspective.

### Meaning
The **Modern Accounting Equation Approach** (also called the American Approach) is built on the fundamental **Accounting Equation**:

$$\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Capital}$$

When we expand Capital to include profit components (Revenues increase capital; Expenses decrease capital), we obtain **five modern account categories**:
1. **Assets** (e.g., Cash, Bank, Furniture, Machinery, Debtors, Prepaid Expenses)
2. **Liabilities** (e.g., Creditors, Bank Loan, Outstanding Expenses)
3. **Capital / Owner's Equity** (e.g., Capital A/c; Drawings reduces Capital)
4. **Expenses / Losses** (e.g., Purchases, Salary, Rent, Depreciation)
5. **Revenues / Gains** (e.g., Sales, Commission Received, Interest Received)

### Why It Is Important
The Modern Approach explains *why* the Balance Sheet always stays balanced after every transaction. It is widely used in international accounting standards (IFRS) and computerised ERP systems.

### Rules of Debit and Credit under the Modern Approach

Notice the clean symmetry: **Assets and Expenses** follow one pattern; **Liabilities, Capital, and Revenues** follow the exact opposite pattern.

| Modern Account Category | When It INCREASES (+) | When It DECREASES (−) |
| :--- | :---: | :---: |
| **1. Assets** | **Debit (`Dr.`)** | **Credit (`Cr.`)** |
| **2. Expenses / Losses** | **Debit (`Dr.`)** | **Credit (`Cr.`)** |
| **3. Liabilities** | **Credit (`Cr.`)** | **Debit (`Dr.`)** |
| **4. Capital (Equity)** | **Credit (`Cr.`)** | **Debit (`Dr.`)** |
| **5. Revenues / Gains** | **Credit (`Cr.`)** | **Debit (`Dr.`)** |

*(Helpful Memory Acronym: **A-E** increases with Debit; **L-C-R** increases with Credit.)*

### Simple Example (Modern Approach Only)
**Transaction:** Paid ₹15,000 cash to settle a supplier's bill (Creditor: *Nikhil Traders*).
- **Nikhil Traders (Liability):** Liability decreases by ₹15,000 → **Debit Nikhil Traders A/c ₹15,000**.
- **Cash (Asset):** Asset decreases by ₹15,000 → **Credit Cash A/c ₹15,000**.
- **Equation Check:** Assets (−₹15,000) = Liabilities (−₹15,000) + Capital (₹0). Equation stays balanced!

### Practical Accounting Application
Whether an accountant uses the **Traditional Approach** or the **Modern Approach**, the final Journal Entry is **always 100% identical**! In the rest of this chapter, we return exclusively to our primary **Traditional Approach**.

## 9. Steps for Recording Journal Entries

Follow this reliable **5-Step Checklist** for every transaction until it becomes second nature:

1. **Step 1 – Read the Transaction Carefully:** Identify the date, the exact amount in ₹, whether it is a cash, bank, credit, or adjustment transaction, and whether it involves *goods* (for resale) or a *fixed asset* (for long-term business use).
2. **Step 2 – Identify the Two (or More) Accounts Involved:** Ask *"What are the two distinct accounting aspects affected?"*
   - *Tip:* If a person's name is mentioned **and** cash/bank payment is also mentioned (e.g., *"Paid rent ₹10,000 to landlord Mr. Sharma in cash"*), the transaction is a **cash transaction**—ignore the person's name and use `Rent A/c` and `Cash A/c`. Use a person's name **only** in credit transactions or when settling an existing credit balance.
3. **Step 3 – Classify Each Account (Traditional Approach):** Label each account strictly as **Personal** (Natural / Artificial / Representative), **Real** (Tangible / Intangible), or **Nominal**.
4. **Step 4 – Apply the Three Golden Rules:** Apply the exact rule for each account to decide which account is **Debited** and which is **Credited**.
5. **Step 5 – Write the Journal Entry, Narration & Balance Check:** Enter the date, Debit line (`... A/c Dr.`), indented Credit line (`To ... A/c`), concise Narration `(Being ...)`, and verify that `Total Debit (₹) = Total Credit (₹)`.

## 10. Format of Journal and Narration

### Standard Format of a Journal

**In the Books of [Business Name]**  
**Journal**

| Date | Particulars | L.F. | Debit (₹) | Credit (₹) |
| :--- | :--- | :---: | ---: | ---: |
| 2025 Apr 1 | Cash A/c &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Dr.<br>&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;To Capital A/c<br>*(Being business commenced with cash)* | | 3,00,000 | <br>3,00,000 |

### Meaning and Purpose of Each Column
1. **Date:** Records the year, month, and day of the transaction chronologically.
2. **Particulars:** Contains three lines for every entry:
   - **Line 1 (Debit Account):** Written close to the left margin, ending with `Dr.`
   - **Line 2 (Credit Account):** Slightly indented to the right, beginning with the word `To`.
   - **Line 3 (Narration):** Written inside brackets `(Being ...)` immediately below the entry.
3. **L.F. (Ledger Folio):** Stands for the page number in the Ledger where this entry is later posted. It is left blank during journalising and filled in during ledger posting.
4. **Debit (₹):** Records the monetary amount debited against the Debit account line.
5. **Credit (₹):** Records the monetary amount credited against the Credit account line.

### Meaning and Importance of Narration
A **Narration** is a brief, clear explanation of the transaction written in parentheses immediately after each journal entry, traditionally beginning with the word **"Being"** (or *"For"*).
- **Why it is important:** Without narration, an entry like `Drawings A/c Dr. To Purchases A/c` does not explain *why* purchases were credited. Adding `(Being goods withdrawn by the proprietor for personal use)` makes the record self-explanatory for auditors and exam evaluators.

### Simple vs. Compound Journal Entries
- **Simple Journal Entry:** Involves exactly **one Debit** and **one Credit** (e.g., `Rent A/c Dr. To Cash A/c`).
- **Compound Journal Entry:** When two or more related transactions occur on the **same date** and share a common Debit or Credit account, they can be combined into a single entry with multiple debits and/or multiple credits—as long as **Total Debits = Total Credits**.
  - *Practical Example:* On April 30, paying ₹10,000 for Shop Rent and ₹2,500 for Electricity Charges together via a single bank cheque of ₹12,500:
    - `Rent A/c Dr. ₹10,000`
    - `Electricity Charges A/c Dr. ₹2,500`
    - `To Bank A/c ₹12,500`
    - *(Check: ₹10,000 + ₹2,500 = ₹12,500 — mathematically balanced!)*

## 11. Five Worked Business Transactions

Let us apply our 5-Step Method to five realistic transactions of **Mehta Stationery & Office Mart** (Proprietor: Mr. Vikram Mehta, Pune) for **April 2025**.

---

### Worked Example 1: Commencing Business with Cash

- **Business Transaction:** On April 1, 2025, Mr. Vikram Mehta commenced his stationery business with cash ₹3,00,000.
- **Accounts Involved:**
  1. Cash A/c
  2. Capital A/c
- **Account Classification (Traditional Approach):**
  1. **Cash A/c** → Tangible Real Account
  2. **Capital A/c** → Representative Personal Account *(represents the proprietor, Mr. Vikram Mehta)*
- **Debit and Credit Reasoning:**
  1. **Cash A/c (Real Account):** Rule — *Debit what comes in*. Cash of ₹3,00,000 comes into the business → **Debit Cash A/c**.
  2. **Capital A/c (Personal Account):** Rule — *Credit the giver*. Under the Business Entity Concept, the proprietor is the giver of funds to the business → **Credit Capital A/c**.
- **Journal Entry:**

| Date | Particulars | L.F. | Debit (₹) | Credit (₹) |
| :--- | :--- | :---: | ---: | ---: |
| 2025 Apr 1 | Cash A/c &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Dr.<br>&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;To Capital A/c<br>*(Being business commenced with cash by the proprietor)* | | 3,00,000 | <br>3,00,000 |

- **Narration:** *(Being business commenced with cash by the proprietor)*
- **Explanation:** Accounting always looks at transactions from the **business's point of view**, not the owner's personal viewpoint. The business received ₹3,00,000 in physical cash (Real Account coming in) and now owes that capital back to the proprietor (Personal Account as giver). Total Debits (₹3,00,000) = Total Credits (₹3,00,000).

---

### Worked Example 2: Depositing Cash into Bank

- **Business Transaction:** On April 3, 2025, Mehta Stationery & Office Mart deposited ₹1,20,000 cash into its business bank account with State Bank of India.
- **Accounts Involved:**
  1. Bank A/c
  2. Cash A/c
- **Account Classification (Traditional Approach):**
  1. **Bank A/c** → Artificial Personal Account
  2. **Cash A/c** → Tangible Real Account
- **Debit and Credit Reasoning:**
  1. **Bank A/c (Personal Account):** Rule — *Debit the receiver*. The bank receives ₹1,20,000 from the business → **Debit Bank A/c**.
  2. **Cash A/c (Real Account):** Rule — *Credit what goes out*. Physical cash of ₹1,20,000 goes out of the business cash box → **Credit Cash A/c**.
- **Journal Entry:**

| Date | Particulars | L.F. | Debit (₹) | Credit (₹) |
| :--- | :--- | :---: | ---: | ---: |
| 2025 Apr 3 | Bank A/c &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Dr.<br>&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;To Cash A/c<br>*(Being cash deposited into the business bank account)* | | 1,20,000 | <br>1,20,000 |

- **Narration:** *(Being cash deposited into the business bank account)*
- **Explanation:** Notice how cleanly the Personal and Real rules work together here. The business did not spend or lose money—it simply moved ₹1,20,000 from its cash drawer (Real A/c going out) to the bank (Artificial Personal A/c receiving the funds).

### Worked Example 3: Credit Purchase of Goods

- **Business Transaction:** On April 6, 2025, purchased stationery goods worth ₹45,000 on credit from Saraswati Paper Mart Pvt. Ltd.
- **Accounts Involved:**
  1. Purchases A/c
  2. Saraswati Paper Mart Pvt. Ltd. A/c
- **Account Classification (Traditional Approach):**
  1. **Purchases A/c** → Nominal Account *(expense of acquiring goods for resale)*
  2. **Saraswati Paper Mart Pvt. Ltd. A/c** → Artificial Personal Account *(supplier company)*
- **Debit and Credit Reasoning:**
  1. **Purchases A/c (Nominal Account):** Rule — *Debit all expenses and losses*. Purchasing trading goods is a business expense → **Debit Purchases A/c**.
  2. **Saraswati Paper Mart Pvt. Ltd. A/c (Personal Account):** Rule — *Credit the giver*. The supplier company is the giver of goods on credit → **Credit Saraswati Paper Mart Pvt. Ltd. A/c**.
- **Journal Entry:**

| Date | Particulars | L.F. | Debit (₹) | Credit (₹) |
| :--- | :--- | :---: | ---: | ---: |
| 2025 Apr 6 | Purchases A/c &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Dr.<br>&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;To Saraswati Paper Mart Pvt. Ltd. A/c<br>*(Being stationery goods purchased on credit from Saraswati Paper Mart Pvt. Ltd.)* | | 45,000 | <br>45,000 |

- **Narration:** *(Being stationery goods purchased on credit from Saraswati Paper Mart Pvt. Ltd.)*
- **Explanation:** Because no cash or cheque was paid immediately, Cash A/c and Bank A/c are not affected. Instead, the supplier's Artificial Personal Account is credited to record our obligation (Creditor) to pay ₹45,000 in the future. Also note that we use **Purchases A/c** (not "Goods A/c") whenever items meant for resale are bought.

### Worked Example 4: Payment of Business Expense in Cash

- **Business Transaction:** On April 10, 2025, paid shop rent of ₹18,000 in cash to the landlord, Mr. Deshmukh.
- **Accounts Involved:**
  1. Rent A/c
  2. Cash A/c
- **Account Classification (Traditional Approach):**
  1. **Rent A/c** → Nominal Account
  2. **Cash A/c** → Tangible Real Account
- **Debit and Credit Reasoning:**
  1. **Rent A/c (Nominal Account):** Rule — *Debit all expenses and losses*. Shop rent is an operating expense of the business → **Debit Rent A/c**.
  2. **Cash A/c (Real Account):** Rule — *Credit what goes out*. Cash of ₹18,000 goes out of the business → **Credit Cash A/c**.
- **Journal Entry:**

| Date | Particulars | L.F. | Debit (₹) | Credit (₹) |
| :--- | :--- | :---: | ---: | ---: |
| 2025 Apr 10 | Rent A/c &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Dr.<br>&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;To Cash A/c<br>*(Being shop rent paid in cash)* | | 18,000 | <br>18,000 |

- **Narration:** *(Being shop rent paid in cash)*
- **Explanation:** Even though the landlord's name (*Mr. Deshmukh*) appears in the transaction, we **do not** debit Mr. Deshmukh's A/c! When an expense is paid immediately in cash or by bank, we record the **name of the expense** (`Rent A/c`), not the name of the person who received payment.

### Worked Example 5: Receipt of Commission by Cheque

- **Business Transaction:** On April 15, 2025, received commission of ₹7,500 by cheque for referring a corporate printing order, and deposited the cheque into the bank on the same day.
- **Accounts Involved:**
  1. Bank A/c
  2. Commission Received A/c
- **Account Classification (Traditional Approach):**
  1. **Bank A/c** → Artificial Personal Account
  2. **Commission Received A/c** → Nominal Account
- **Debit and Credit Reasoning:**
  1. **Bank A/c (Personal Account):** Rule — *Debit the receiver*. The bank receives the cheque amount of ₹7,500 on behalf of the business → **Debit Bank A/c**.
  2. **Commission Received A/c (Nominal Account):** Rule — *Credit all incomes and gains*. Commission earned is an income for the business → **Credit Commission Received A/c**.
- **Journal Entry:**

| Date | Particulars | L.F. | Debit (₹) | Credit (₹) |
| :--- | :--- | :---: | ---: | ---: |
| 2025 Apr 15 | Bank A/c &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Dr.<br>&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;To Commission Received A/c<br>*(Being commission received by cheque and deposited into bank)* | | 7,500 | <br>7,500 |

- **Narration:** *(Being commission received by cheque and deposited into bank)*
- **Explanation:** Because the cheque was deposited into the bank on the same day, **Bank A/c** is debited directly (not Cash A/c). Specifying `Commission Received A/c` (rather than just "Commission A/c") makes it unmistakable that this Nominal Account represents an income rather than an expense.

## 12. Common Mistakes Made by Students

Watch out for these **seven classic beginner mistakes** in First-Year B.Com exams:

| # | Common Mistake | Why It Is Wrong | Correct Treatment |
| :-: | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1** | **Debiting `Purchases A/c` when buying a Fixed Asset** (e.g., Machinery, Furniture, Computer). | `Purchases A/c` is **only** for goods bought for resale. Assets bought for long-term business use must be recorded in their own Real Account. | Debit `Machinery A/c` / `Furniture A/c` (NOT `Purchases A/c`). |
| **2** | **Classifying `Bank A/c` as a Real Account.** | Cash is tangible currency (Real), whereas a Bank is an institution created by law. | Always classify `Bank A/c` as an **Artificial Personal Account** under the Traditional Approach. |
| **3** | **Using a person's name for Cash/Bank expense payments** (e.g., debiting *Landlord's A/c* instead of *Rent A/c*). | Immediate expense payments do not create a debtor/creditor relationship with the payee. | Always debit the **Nominal Expense Account** (e.g., `Rent A/c`, `Salary A/c`). |
| **4** | **Treating Owner's Personal Expenses as Business Expenses** (e.g., paying the owner's daughter's college fees or home electricity bill from business cash and debiting `Education Expense A/c`). | Violates the **Business Entity Concept**. Personal expenses of the proprietor are withdrawals of owner's funds, not business operating expenses. | Debit **`Drawings A/c`** (Representative Personal A/c) and Credit `Cash A/c` (or `Bank A/c`). |
| **5** | **Crediting `Sales A/c` when goods are withdrawn by the owner for personal use.** | Goods withdrawn by the owner are taken at **cost price**, not sold to a customer for profit. Crediting `Sales A/c` would falsely inflate sales revenue. | Debit `Drawings A/c` and Credit **`Purchases A/c`** (reducing the cost of goods available for resale). |
| **6** | **Confusing `Outstanding` / `Prepaid` accounts as Nominal Accounts.** | While `Salary A/c` and `Insurance A/c` are Nominal, adding *Outstanding, Prepaid, Accrued,* or *Unearned* turns them into accounts representing people/parties. | Classify all Outstanding, Prepaid, Accrued, and Unearned accounts as **Representative Personal Accounts**. |
| **7** | **Skipping Narration or forgetting to check Debit = Credit** in compound entries. | Omitting narration costs easy exam marks and weakens the audit trail; unbalanced entries break double-entry accounting. | Always write `(Being ...)` and verify that the sum of Debit column equals the sum of Credit column. |

## 13. Important Concepts and Quick Revision

Use this compact reference sheet before practicing exercises or taking exams:

### A. Traditional Classification & Golden Rules at a Glance
| Account Type | Subtypes | Golden Rule | Key Examples |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Personal** | Natural, Artificial, Representative | **Dr.** the Receiver / **Cr.** the Giver | *Ramesh A/c, Bank A/c, Capital A/c, Drawings A/c, Outstanding Salary A/c, Prepaid Insurance A/c, Accrued Commission A/c* |
| **Real** | Tangible, Intangible | **Dr.** what Comes In / **Cr.** what Goes Out | *Cash A/c, Furniture A/c, Machinery A/c, Goodwill A/c, Patents A/c* |
| **Nominal** | Expenses & Losses; Incomes & Gains | **Dr.** Expenses & Losses / **Cr.** Incomes & Gains | *Purchases A/c, Sales A/c, Salary A/c, Rent A/c, Depreciation A/c, Commission Received A/c* |

### B. Essential Special Entries Every B.Com Student Must Know
1. **Cash Withdrawn from Bank for Office Use:** `Cash A/c Dr. To Bank A/c` *(Cash comes into office; Bank is the giver—this is NOT Drawings!)*
2. **Cash Withdrawn by Owner for Personal Use:** `Drawings A/c Dr. To Cash A/c` (or `To Bank A/c`) *(Owner is receiver; Cash/Bank goes out/gives)*
3. **Goods Withdrawn by Owner for Personal Use:** `Drawings A/c Dr. To Purchases A/c` *(Owner is receiver; Purchase expense is reduced)*
4. **Outstanding (Unpaid) Expense at Period-End:** `Expense A/c Dr. To Outstanding Expense A/c` *(Expense incurred is debited; Representative Personal Account for payable party is credited)*
5. **Prepaid (Paid-in-Advance) Expense Adjustment at Year-End:** `Prepaid Expense A/c Dr. To Expense A/c` *(Representative Personal Account representing the party who owes future service is debited as receiver; current year's Nominal Expense is reduced by crediting it)*
6. **Annual Depreciation on Fixed Asset:** `Depreciation A/c Dr. To [Asset] A/c` *(Depreciation is a Nominal non-cash loss/expense debited; Tangible Real Asset value reduces/goes out so Asset A/c is credited)*

### C. Traditional vs. Modern Approach — Never Mix Them!
- **Traditional Approach:** Personal, Real, Nominal → Three Golden Rules.
- **Modern Approach:** Assets, Liabilities, Capital, Expenses, Revenues → Increase/Decrease Rules (`Assets & Expenses: +Dr / −Cr`; `Liabilities, Capital & Revenues: +Cr / −Dr`).
- **Golden Guarantee:** Both methods always produce the **exact same Journal Entry**.

## 14. Chapter Summary

- A **Journal** is the **Book of Original Entry** where all financial transactions are recorded chronologically in double-entry form before posting to the Ledger.
- Every transaction has a **dual aspect**, ensuring **Total Debits always equal Total Credits** in every simple or compound journal entry.
- Under the **Traditional Approach**, accounts are divided into **Personal** (Natural, Artificial, Representative), **Real** (Tangible, Intangible), and **Nominal** Accounts, governed by the **Three Golden Rules of Accounting**.
- Under the **Modern Accounting Equation Approach** (`Assets = Liabilities + Capital`), accounts are grouped into five categories—Assets, Liabilities, Capital, Expenses, and Revenues—which provide an independent check on debit and credit logic.
- Accurate journalising requires following the **5-Step Process**: reading carefully, identifying accounts, classifying accurately, applying the Golden Rules, and formatting the entry cleanly with a concise **Narration**.
- Mastering Journal Entries lays the unshakeable foundation for Ledger Posting, Trial Balance, and Final Accounts in First-Year B.Com Financial Accounting.
